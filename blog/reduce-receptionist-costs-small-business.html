How to Reduce Receptionist Costs in Your Small Business

For many small business owners in Australia, a receptionist feels essential. Someone to answer the phone, book appointments, greet clients, handle the day-to-day call volume. But the cost of keeping that role filled is significant, and for businesses running lean, it is often the first expense that gets scrutinised.

If you are looking at your wage bill and wondering whether there is a smarter way to handle incoming calls without sacrificing the customer experience, this article is for you. We will walk through the real costs involved, the options available, and how to think through the right fit for your business.

What Does a Receptionist Actually Cost an Australian Business?

The base wage is just the starting point. In 2025, a full-time receptionist in Australia typically earns between $55,000 and $65,000 per year depending on your location and industry. Add superannuation at 11.5%, payroll tax if you are above the threshold, annual leave loading, sick leave, and the cost of cover when they are away, and the real annual cost often sits closer to $75,000 or more.

Part-time arrangements reduce that figure, but they also reduce coverage. A receptionist working school hours does not help when a client rings at 7am or after 5pm. And missed calls have their own cost, which is easy to underestimate. Research consistently shows that a large proportion of callers who reach voicemail simply do not try again, which means missed revenue, not just missed calls. We covered this in more detail in our article on whether customers call back if no one answers.

Where the Hidden Costs Add Up

Beyond wages, there are costs most owners do not factor in at the start:

  • Recruitment and onboarding: Finding, interviewing, and training a new receptionist takes time and money. If the role turns over, that cost repeats.
  • Downtime: Lunch breaks, holidays, sick days, and staff meetings all create gaps in phone coverage.
  • Inconsistency: Different staff members handle calls differently. A tired or distracted receptionist can leave a poor impression that costs you a client.
  • Office overhead: A receptionist needs a desk, a computer, and a phone setup. For businesses in commercial leases, that physical footprint has a dollar value.

None of these costs are reasons to never hire a receptionist. But they are worth understanding clearly before you decide whether your current setup is working for you.

Options for Reducing Receptionist Costs Without Dropping Standards

There are several approaches Australian businesses commonly take. Each has trade-offs.

Reduce Hours and Use Voicemail as a Backup

Cutting receptionist hours is the obvious move, but voicemail is a poor substitute. Most callers, particularly those ringing a trade, clinic, or service business for the first time, hang up when they hit a recorded message and call a competitor instead. If your business depends on converting inbound enquiries, voicemail is not a reliable safety net.

Use a Human Answering Service

Human answering services can take overflow or after-hours calls on your behalf. Pricing varies widely, but expect to pay per call or per minute, which can add up quickly if your call volume is moderate or unpredictable. For a comparison of these options, see our piece on virtual receptionist vs answering service.

Redirect Calls to a Team Member

In very small businesses, the owner or a senior staff member sometimes takes on call handling themselves. This works until it does not. Interruptions during client work, on-site jobs, or consultations create their own problems, both for quality of work and for the impression left on the caller.

Use an AI Phone Receptionist

AI voice receptionists have improved significantly in recent years and are now a practical option for Australian small businesses. Unlike chatbots or phone trees, a well-built AI receptionist holds a natural conversation, answers questions about your business, and can book appointments in real time. The cost is typically a flat monthly fee, which makes budgeting straightforward.

What AI Receptionists Can and Cannot Do

Being honest about this matters. An AI receptionist handles call answering, business FAQs, appointment booking, and SMS confirmations well. It is available around the clock, never calls in sick, and delivers a consistent experience every time.

What it does not do is handle complex disputes, provide detailed professional advice, or replace a person for tasks that require physical presence or nuanced human judgement. For most small businesses, the call types that need that level of handling are a minority. Which Businesses Benefit Most

Reducing receptionist costs with AI works best for businesses where most calls follow a predictable pattern. Think:

  • Trade businesses where the owner is often on site and cannot take calls during work hours. See our guide on handling business calls on a job site.
  • Salons, clinics, and allied health practices with high appointment booking volumes. Relevant reading: how to stop missing calls at your salon and answering services for medical clinics.
  • Solo operators and small teams who cannot justify a full-time salary but are losing business to missed calls.
  • Businesses that get enquiries outside business hours and currently have no way to capture them.

If your call volume is high and you have complex, varied enquiries that require significant back-and-forth with a human, a hybrid model might suit you better: AI for routine calls and after-hours, with a staff member handling escalations during business hours.
